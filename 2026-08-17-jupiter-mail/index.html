the wind ladder

courses you can hold

① a balloon has no rudder — altitude is the steering wheel

Near the ground, friction drags the wind left of its high-altitude self and slows it (an idealized Ekman spiral): with ζ = (z + z₀)/δ,

u(ζ) = U_g (1 − e⁻ᶻ cos ζ)   along the geostrophic wind
v(ζ) = U_g e⁻ᶻ sin ζ       to its left (surface wind is “backed”)

So each altitude holds a different course: the set of headings you can fly today is exactly the arc your wind ladder spans — low and slow on the left edge, high and fast on the right. Crawfordsville bears 178° from Lafayette; on Aug 17, 1859 that meant about 1 300 m up.

② a kilo of hydrogen lifts 13.4 kilos — at every altitude

The envelope is open to the air, so the gas stays at ambient pressure and temperature. Lift and the air it displaces then scale together as you climb:

lift = (M_air/M_H₂ − 1) · m_gas · g ≈ 13.4 · m_gas · g

Climbing doesn't change your lift — it swells the envelope (536 m³ of H₂ at release become ~690 m³ at 2 500 m). Which is why the only way down is the valve, the only way up is sand, and the leak (~0.05 kg/min through the fabric) slowly turns a trimmed balloon into a sinking one. The ship also answers like a barge: roughly half the displaced air rides along as added mass, so a bag of ballast takes six seconds just to reach 1 m/s of climb.

③ the mail's real journey was balloon + train

Wise carried the locked bag up at 4:30 pm, read the sky for about an hour, and put Jupiter down near Crawfordsville — 26 miles short of a continent. The postmaster forwarded the letters by rail, and the first US airmail arrived in New York the ordinary way. The verdict panel estimates that second leg the same way: hours to New York fall with every kilometre east you land (a playful 1859-plausible rail model, not an archive). Land soft (≤ 2.5 m/s), near a depot (≤ 5 km), before the evening train — and the trail rope, Wise's own invention, drags on the ground to brake you and hold a stable low altitude for the hand-over.
