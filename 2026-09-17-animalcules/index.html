The Little Animals

A Leeuwenhoek studio for the anniversary of the letter that described bacteria for the first time: a Delft draper scraped the batter-thick film off his own teeth, looked at it through a bead of glass he had ground himself, and wrote to London about creatures "very prettily a-moving".

Antony van Leeuwenhoek kept a draper's shop, gauged wine for the city, and in his spare time made the best lenses on Earth — tiny single glass beads that magnified 100–275× while Hooke's compound microscopes fogged out past 30×. On 17 September 1683 he sent Francis Aston the letter with the dental plaque drawings. The four machines below run the actual closed-form optics of that bead, the colour/diffraction trade that set its resolution near 1–2 µm, the plaque itself at true scale, and the road from his bench to the electron microscope. MACHINE Ⅰ

A bead of glass

His microscope was a single ball lens, 1–2 mm across, sandwiched between two brass plates with the specimen stuck on a pointed pin in front and a screw to nudge it toward focus. Focus a sphere and the algebra collapses to three lines: focal length, magnification, and a numerical aperture that depends on nothing but the glass index. The ray fan below is traced exactly — Snell's law twice per ray, no paraxial shortcuts — from the specimen at the front focus into the eye's side.

MACHINE Ⅱ

The colour trap

Why did the best lenses of the age stop at 1–2 µm instead of the 0.4 µm diffraction promises? Dispersion. Blue and red focus microns apart, and the smear grows with the cone you accept, while diffraction shrinks with it. Minimising the sum of the two has a closed form — the bead's own sweet spot — and it lands right where the surviving instruments measure.

MACHINE Ⅲ

The scurf of the teeth

The letter of 17 September 1683, live. He sampled himself and four other people — one an old man who had never cleaned his teeth — and found "an unbelievably great company of living animalcules". Below, the plaque ecosystem at true µm scale as his bead would render it: every creature blurred by the machine's own resolution, so you can see exactly why the pike was a darting thread and the smallest sort barely dots. Drop the wine vinegar, as he did, and watch the swimmers lie still.

"…in the said matter there were many very little living animalcules, very prettily a-moving."

"The biggest sort… had a very strong and swift motion, and shot through the water (or spittle) like a pike does through the water."

"The second sort… oft-times spun round like a top… and these were far more in number."

MACHINE Ⅳ

From Delft to the strong room

He never told anyone how he ground the beads. More than 500 lenses, roughly 560 letters, a Fellowship he never collected in person — and then two centuries of quiet until Pasteur and Koch gave the little animals a job. Drag the resolution floor down the years and watch each creature come into view; at the bottom of the ladder, in 1981, Brian Ford opened Leeuwenhoek's original specimen packets in the Royal Society's strong room and re-photographed what was really on his pins.
