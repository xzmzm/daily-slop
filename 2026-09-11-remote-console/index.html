STEP 1: RAW 4-BIT BINARY SUM
(A + 3) + (B + 3) = (7 + 3) + (8 + 3) = 10 + 11 = 21 (Excess-6)
In 4-bit binary, 21 overflows: Raw 4-bit = 5 (0101₂), Carry Out Cout = 1.
STEP 2: STIBITZ EXCESS CORRECTION
Cout = 1 ⇒ Add +3 (0011₂): 5 + 3 = 8 (1000₂)
When sum ≥ 10, binary adder subtracted 16 instead of 10. Adding +3 restores Excess-3 representation!
FINAL RESULT DIGIT
XS-3 Code: 8 (1000₂) ⇒ Decimal Digit: 5 (Carry: 1)
Combined result: 7 + 8 = 15
STIBITZ'S EXCESS-3 THEOREM
WHY 1930s BELL LABS REPLACED STANDARD BCD

1. Self-Complementing 9's Complement

In standard 8421 BCD, 9's complement required complex multi-stage correction logic. In Stibitz XS-3, 9's complement is literally bitwise NOT!

~XS3(D) = ~(D + 3) = 15 - (D + 3) = (9 - D) + 3 = XS3(9 - D)

Stibitz simply swapped the normally-open (NO) and normally-closed (NC) relay contacts—zero extra relays required for decimal subtraction!

2. Line Fault & Wire-Break Detection

In standard BCD, decimal 0 is encoded as 0000₂. A broken wire or disconnected relay also yields 0000₂, masking fatal hardware faults.

In Excess-3, decimal 0 is 0011₂. The states 0000₂ and 1111₂ are completely invalid—any open circuit or stuck relay triggers an immediate hardware alarm!

NOVEMBER 1937: THE "MODEL K" ON THE KITCHEN TABLE
BOONTON, NEW JERSEY · GEORGE R. STIBITZ

In late autumn 1937, Bell Labs mathematician George Stibitz observed that telephone relays functioned as two-state Boolean devices. He brought home two surplus telephone relays, a dry cell battery, flashlight bulbs, and strips of a tin can cut with kitchen shears. That evening on his kitchen table, he assembled the world's first electromechanical binary 1-bit adder:

“I observed that relays were binary in nature... They were either open or closed. I asked myself whether relays could perform arithmetic. My wife and I called it the Model K, for kitchen table.”
— Dr. George R. Stibitz (1904–1995)
TELECOMPUTING & RELAY COMPUTERS TIMELINE
1937 – 1964
NOV 1937

Model K Kitchen Table Adder

George Stibitz builds 1-bit binary adder with 2 Bell relays, dry cells, flashlight bulbs, and cut tobacco tin can in Boonton, NJ.

FEB 1938

Bell Labs Research Memo

Stibitz submits proposal to Bell Labs management demonstrating how telephone relay networks can solve complex number filter equations for carrier telephony.

NOV 1939

Complex Number Calculator (Model I) Completed

Designed by Stibitz and engineered by Samuel B. Williams. 450 relays and 10 crossbar switches installed in Room 914, 463 West Street, Manhattan.

11 SEP 1940 ★

Dartmouth College Demonstration (World First)

At the AMS meeting in Hanover, NH, Stibitz connects a Model 14 Teletype over 250 miles of telegraph wire to the NYC calculator. Norbert Wiener and John von Neumann attend; remote computing is born.

1941–1945

Wartime Relay Computers (Model II, III, IV)

Specialized relay computers developed for National Defense Research Committee (NDRC) to test the M-9 anti-aircraft gun director and fire control tables.

1946

Model V Relay Computer

First general-purpose, fully programmable floating-point relay computer with dual processing units and paper tape CADET diagnostic system.

1964

Stibitz Joins Dartmouth Medical Faculty

Dr. Stibitz returns to Dartmouth College as professor of physiology, pioneering computer modeling of renal physiology and pharmacokinetics.
