03 THE CHECK DIGIT

MOD 10 · WEIGHTS 3·1

Positions counted from the left: odd positions count three times, even positions once, and the check digit tops the sum up to the next ten. The rule catches every single-digit misprint — and nine of every ten swapped neighbours.

04 THE 1952 BULLSEYE

ORIENTATION-PROOF BY DESIGN

Woodland’s 1952 patent drew concentric rings, not bars: a ray out of the centre crosses the same black-white sequence at any angle, so the scanner never needs to know which way is up. RCA built it — and offset presses kept smearing ink into the bullseye’s centre, so the straight-line code won the 1973 selection instead. These rings carry today’s 95 UPC modules, innermost first.

WHY TODAY?
On 7 October 1952 the US Patent Office granted Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver No. 2,612,994, “Classifying Apparatus and Method” — the first bar code patent. It began in 1948, when a supermarket president asked Drexel’s dean for automated checkout and Silver overheard the refusal. Woodland, thinking of Morse code, drew four lines in the sand of a Miami beach. The patent sold to Philco for $15,000; Silver died in 1963, a decade before the first scan.

THE FIRST SCAN
26 June 1974, Marsh Supermarket, Troy, Ohio: a ten-pack of Wrigley’s Juicy Fruit became the first product ever scanned with a UPC. The gum now sits in the Smithsonian. A UPC-A carries 12 digits in 95 modules — number system, maker, item, and a check digit that refuses every single-digit misprint.
