43 YEARS AGO TODAY · THE WINDOWS 1.0 ANNOUNCEMENT

No overlap.

Microsoft picked 10 November 1983 to announce Windows at the Plaza Hotel, and gave its desktop one strict rule: windows tile, they never overlap. The current window takes the big tile; everyone else fills a column and a row around it. Drag the sashes, swap the current window, zoom — then break the rule with 1987's overlap. That constraint was memory, not taste: clipped redraws of hidden windows were unaffordable in 1983.

SEQUENCE OF EVENTS

    How the tiling works

    The desktop splits into three zones: the current window takes the big top-left tile, a full-height column on the right holds half of the rest, and a row along the bottom-left holds the others. Sashes only move the zone boundaries — every window position comes from a few stored fractions, so no two windows can ever share a pixel. Zoom gives the current window the whole desktop and sends the others to the icon strip.

    Why no overlap

    Memory. Overlapping windows need clipping: every exposed region of every hidden window redrawn, in the right order, on machines with ~256 KB of RAM and text-mode graphics cards. Tiling meant the system only ever drew the current window, so Windows 1.0 shipped without overlap. Free overlapping windows arrived with Windows 2.0 in December 1987 — four years after the announcement and two years after 1.0 itself shipped.

    What's real

    The date and place (Plaza Hotel, New York, 10 November 1983), the $99 announced price, the 20 November 1985 ship date and the no-overlap rule are real, as are the apps used here — MS-DOS Executive, Clock, Notepad, Calculator and Calendar all shipped with 1.0. Windows 1.0's exact tiling math was never published in detail, so the three-zone layout here is an approximation.
