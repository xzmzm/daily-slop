Falling forever

Sputnik 1 went up at 19:28 UTC and the whole planet could hear it beep. An orbit is just falling and missing — Newton drew the picture in 1687. Bench one proves it with a cannon; bench two is the radio that heard it go by.

I Newton’s cannon Principia, 1687

A cannon on an impossibly tall mountain (we chose 500 km — Newton didn’t say). Fire horizontally. The shot falls toward Earth every second it flies; the only question is whether the ground curves away as fast.

    II The beep 20.005 MHz · 1 watt

    Sputnik’s orbit was 215 × 939 km, one lap every 96 minutes. Anyone with a shortwave receiver could catch a 10-minute pass. The pitch glides as it comes and goes — Doppler: Δf = f₀·vr/c. We mix the carrier down to a ~940 Hz beat note; the Hz shift you hear is exactly the shift the radio waves carried.

    83.6 kg polished sphere, 58 cm across · beeps for 22 days (batteries died 26 Oct) · burned up 4 Jan 1958 · the R-7 core stage orbited beside it and was visible to the naked eye · pass time compressed ≈ 58×, beep cadence slowed to stay audible — the Hz glide is the real thing.

    Simplifications, kept honest: Newton’s mountain height is our choice; Sputnik’s slightly elliptical orbit is modelled as a circle at its mean radius (e = 0.052); pass geometry lives in the plane through the observer; the ±0.7 kHz temperature drift is a slow-sine stand-in for the real thermal wander, which was measured at roughly the same size as the Doppler shift itself.
