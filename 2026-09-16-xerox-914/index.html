16 September 1959 · Sherry-Netherland Hotel, New York · 67 years ago today

The Scorch Machine 914

A Xerox 914 studio for the anniversary of the first plain-paper copy — demonstrated on live television in a hotel ballroom where one of the two machines caught fire, and 650 lb of selenium, corona wire and cascading beads quietly rebuilt the office around a nickel.

Chester Carlson's xerography writes with static electricity and dry powder: charge a selenium drum in the dark, expose it to light (charge flows away wherever light lands), dust it with charged toner, transfer, fuse. No liquid, no negative, no master — plain paper in, plain paper out, seven copies a minute. The four machines below run the actual closed-form physics of that chain, from Peek's-law corona to the triboelectric release threshold, plus the lease arithmetic that let a $27,500 machine into every office for $95 a month. MACHINE Ⅱ

Charge it with lightning, then let light erase

A thin wire at kilovolts breaks the air down (Peek's law) and sprays ions onto the selenium: the drum becomes a capacitor you can see through. Selenium holds the charge in the dark for τ = ρε seconds and conducts it away under light — one absorbed photon, one electron–hole pair, one unit of charge neutralised. Push the wavelength past the 610 nm band edge and selenium goes blind: red light is dark to it.

MACHINE Ⅲ

Beads, dust, and the release threshold

Development is a bucket of glass beads rolling over the drum, each bead wearing a fur of toner charged by triboelectric friction (the same physics as the 1938 handkerchief). Coulomb's law has to beat the toner's own image-force adhesion — which grows with radius — and the toner keeps depositing until its charge screens the field exactly: M/A = ε₀E/(q/m). Too much charge-to-mass and nothing releases (a white copy); too little and the background dirties (a grey one).

MACHINE Ⅳ

The nickel machine

Nobody would buy a $27,500 copier, so Haloid rented it: $95 a month including 2,000 copies, then about 4¢ each — cheaper than a 35¢ Photostat from roughly 49 copies a month. The mimeograph stayed cheaper for long runs of one original; the 914 won everywhere else by skipping the stencil. By 1965 the machine was credited with about two-thirds of Xerox's revenue, and “to xerox” was on its way into the dictionary.
