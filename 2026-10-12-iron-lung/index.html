October 12, 1928 · Boston Children's Hospital

Iron Lung

Polio had paralysed a child's breathing muscles. Philip Drinker and Louis Shaw sealed the child's body in an iron box with the head outside, and two vacuum-cleaner blowers pulled the air pressure in the box up and down. When the pressure around the chest drops, room air flows in through the nose and mouth. Run the pump, and then try it by hand.

Pump
Pressure, not airThe machine never pushes air into the lungs. It lowers the pressure around the body, and the room's own air pressure fills them through the nose and mouth.
50 mL that never arrivesThe last air drawn into each breath stays in the nose, throat and windpipe and is breathed straight back out. Fast shallow breaths can move plenty of air and still bring little to the lungs.
Hand cranks in the wardLater iron lungs came with a hand lever for power cuts. In the polio epidemics of the 1950s, wards held rows of machines, and when the power failed, staff and visitors pumped by hand.

Model: one elastic lung (compliance 15 mL/cmH₂O) behind one airway (20 cmH₂O·s/L) for an eight-year-old of about 25 kg, with the tank pressure acting on the chest wall. Air reaching the lungs is each breath minus 50 mL of dead space, averaged over 4 s; blood CO₂ follows 0.863 × CO₂ production ÷ that ventilation. The blood-gas response is compressed to a 6 s time constant so you can see it; in a real body it takes several minutes.
