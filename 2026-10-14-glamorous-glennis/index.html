October 14, 1947 · Muroc Army Air Field, California

Glamorous Glennis

Captain Chuck Yeager, two cracked ribs taped up, dropped out of a B-29's bomb bay at 20,000 ft in an orange rocket plane named after his wife. Light the four chambers, climb to where sound is slowest, and push through Mach 0.94, where the shock wave takes the elevator away. The fix was the moving tail: trim.

Why 43,000 feetSound travels at the speed of the air's molecules, which depends only on temperature. Above 36,000 ft the air sits at −56.5 °C, so Mach 1 is 660 mph instead of 761 at sea level, and the air is a fifth as dense, so the transonic drag wall is a fifth as tall.
The tail that saved itNear Mach 0.94 a shock forms on the horizontal tail and the elevator behind it stops biting. On the flight four days earlier Yeager lost pitch control there. The X-1's whole stabilizer could be trimmed a fraction of a degree at a time, and the trim still worked when the elevator didn't.
The needle jumpedThe Machmeter stalled around 0.965 and then tipped off the scale as the shock moved past the static port. On the ground the crew heard a sonic boom. The whole supersonic stretch lasted about 20 seconds, and the Air Force kept it secret until June 1948.

Point-mass model: 1976 standard atmosphere, four on/off chambers (1,500 lbf at sea level, about 1,680 lbf in thin air, 3.4 kg/s each), a zero-lift drag coefficient that quadruples between Mach 0.80 and 1.02, induced drag from the load factor, and a pitch channel where the elevator loses 88% of its authority from 0.88 to 0.94 while a shock-induced nose-down load builds. The stabilizer trim keeps full authority. The aircraft and wavefront scales differ, so the X-1 is drawn far larger than its 31 ft. Wavefronts are drawn to scale with the speed of sound.
