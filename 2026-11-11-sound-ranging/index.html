The Science Behind Bragg's Sound Ranging (1915–1918)

The Tucker Hot-Wire Microphone

Standard carbon microphones were deafened by rifle crack and wind. Captain William Sansome Tucker solved this with a tiny platinum wire heated red-hot by an electric battery, housed in an ammunition case neck. High-frequency rifle fire produced little air motion, but the deep ~10 Hz infrasonic pulse of heavy artillery pushed air across the wire, cooling it instantly and changing its electrical resistance.

The Bull String Galvanometer

Developed by Lucien Bull in Paris, five thin silvered quartz strings were suspended in a strong magnetic field. Each connected through copper wire to a forward microphone. An arc lamp projected the shadow of the vibrating strings onto a motor-driven strip of photographic paper. A 100 Hz tuning fork cast time lines across the tape every 0.01 seconds.

Hyperbolas & Wind Corrections

Sound travels outwards in a sphere. The time difference Δt between any pair of microphones defines a hyperbola of constant distance difference Δd = c · Δt. The intersection of three or more hyperbolas pinpoints the gun. William Lawrence Bragg developed field charts correcting for air temperature and wind vectors, turning artillery spotting from rough guesswork into 25-meter accuracy.

The 11th Hour (Armistice Day)

At 11:00 AM on 11 November 1918, the Armistice took effect. The American and British sound ranging film strips recorded the frantic barrage in the minutes before 11:00, followed by the sudden, eerie flattening of all five traces into unbroken horizontal lines—the physical proof that the Great War had ended.
