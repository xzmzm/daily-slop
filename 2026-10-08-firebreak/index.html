EMBER REACH—
CLEARS THE RIVER—

Embers loft on the fire's convection column and land a Rayleigh-distributed distance downwind — the distance grows with the wind, and once enough of the city burns, the fire's own updraft stretches it further. Water stops the flame front; it has never stopped the ember rain.

THE NIGHT

About 9 PM on Sunday, October 8, 1871, fire broke out in or near the De Koven Street barn of Patrick and Catherine O'Leary. The cow-kicked-lantern story was invented by a reporter — Michael Ahern confessed to making it up in 1893 — and the O'Learys were formally exonerated. What is certain: a month-long drought, a city two-thirds built of wood — buildings, sidewalks, streets — and a hot southwesterly gale.

WHY NOTHING STOPPED IT

Firefighters blew up buildings with gunpowder to cut firebreaks; the blasts often failed and sometimes started new fires of their own. Just after midnight burning shingles crossed the south branch of the river; by dawn the fire had jumped the main stem too. The front ran out of city before it ran out of wind — what finally ended it was rain, late Monday night, about 27 hours in: roughly 300 dead, 100,000 homeless, 17,500 buildings, 3.3 square miles.

THE SAME COLD FRONT

The same wind that night drove the Peshtigo fire in Wisconsin — the deadliest fire in United States history, killing at least 1,500 people in an hour — plus fatal fires around Holland and Port Huron, Michigan. Chicago rebuilt in brick and stone, rewrote its fire codes, and Fire Prevention Week is still observed on this anniversary. The Water Works tower on the near north side stood through it; it's still standing.
