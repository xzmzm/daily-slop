Starting point

Constant-Q equilibrium tide (Goldreich–Soter). Neptune's k₂/Q is set so Triton reaches the Roche limit in 3.6 Gyr, inside the published 1–4 Gyr window; the capture preset assumes a young, ocean-bearing Triton. Ghost ellipses are the orbit's own past.

01Found by a brewer, 17 days late

Neptune itself was found on 23 September 1846, in Berlin, from Le Verrier's predicted position. Seventeen nights later William Lassell — a Liverpool brewer who paid for his telescopes with beer money — spotted a moon orbiting the new planet, the first anyone had ever seen there.

It circled the wrong way. That single detail is why Neptune's axis is tilted 28° and why its moons are so few and strange: Triton was a captured visitor, and capture is violent.

02Backwards is a one-way ticket

Neptune spins once every 16 hours; Triton orbits against that spin every 5.9 days. Each pass drags Neptune's tidal bulge the wrong way, and the bulge tugs back: the orbit must shrink. There is no stable retrograde parking spot.

Prograde moons beyond the synchronous line (3.4 RN) drift outward — Earth's Moon gains 3.8 cm a year. Flip Triton prograde in the panel and its doom evaporates into a slow escape.

03The ring Neptune is owed

At the Roche limit — 2.3 RN, about 56,000 km above the center — Neptune's pull overwhelms Triton's own gravity and the moon comes apart. The debris: a ring of 2.1×10²² kg, over a thousand times the mass of Saturn's rings today.

The inner edge rains onto Neptune while the outer edge, safely past the limit, buds back into new moons. Let the clock run far enough and you'll meet them.
