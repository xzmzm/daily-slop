Forty-Four Thousand One Hundred

On this day the Sony CDP-101 — the first compact-disc player — reached shelves alongside fifty launch titles, Billy Joel's 52nd Street among them. A CD is two numbers wearing a silver coat: 44,100 samples a second, 16 bits each. This workbench runs the whole chain — fold an alias, count the bits, work out where 44,100 came from, then scratch the disc and let the parity put it back.

Early digital audio lived on PCM adapters plugged into video tape machines — stereo samples rode the scan lines. 44,100 is the one rate both TV worlds can build: 3 × 245 × 60 in NTSC Japan and the US, 3 × 294 × 50 in PAL Europe. The ear wanted a bit above 40 kHz (20 kHz audio plus a filter guard band); the VTR delivered exactly this, so it stuck — and every "48 kHz or 44.1 kHz" menu today still carries the echo.

The motor never holds one speed: it slows from 458 rpm at the inner edge to 198 at the outer so the track always passes at 1.2 m/s. The spiral is 1.6 µm wide — 20,625 turns, about 5.4 km of track on 120 mm of plastic. The 780 nm laser's ~1.7 µm spot is wider than the 0.5 µm pits it reads; the disc is read by interference, not by a needle.

With interleave on, consecutive disc cells belong to different rows — an 8-cell burst is one lost byte per row, and each row's parity rebuilds its single hole. Turn interleave off and the same burst erases a run of neighbours in one row — and a row with two or more holes loses them all, because one parity byte can only settle one unknown. A real CD plays the same trick with two Reed–Solomon layers (C1 and C2) cross-interleaved over 108 frames — enough to sew up a 2.5 mm gouge without a click.

Beethoven's ninth, 12 centimetres

The compact disc was a Dutch–Japanese joint invention: Philips brought the laser disc optics, Sony the digital signal chain, and in 1982 the standard froze at 120 mm, 44.1 kHz, 16 bits, 74 minutes. Philips had wanted an 11.5 cm, 60-minute disc; Sony's Norio Ohga — a trained opera singer — insisted the format hold Beethoven's ninth symphony whole, and the legend says the 1951 Bayreuth recording, about 74 minutes, drew the diameter. Whether or not the story is precisely true, the CDP-101 that went on sale in Tokyo on October 1, 1982 for ¥168,000 played fifty launch titles that day, the first being 52nd Street. Forty-four years on, 44,100 is still the number a phone reaches for first.
